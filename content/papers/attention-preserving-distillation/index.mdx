## Abstract

Sample content — this demonstration paper exists to exercise the PaperStack pipeline and will be replaced by real research. We ask whether preserving the attention geometry of a teacher transformer during knowledge distillation produces smaller models that stay calibrated under distribution shift. Across three compression ratios, attention-aligned students retain 96.4 percent of teacher accuracy with 31 percent fewer parameters than a token-level baseline, and degrade more gracefully on out-of-domain text.

## Introduction

Compact language models are attractive wherever inference must happen on constrained hardware, but standard distillation objectives treat the teacher's output distribution as the only source of supervision. Work on compression-induced drift suggests that the internal relational structure of transformers degrades faster than the output distribution alone can capture.

This paper makes three contributions:

- An attention-alignment loss that matches student attention entropy to teacher attention entropy at matched layer depths.
- A controlled study across three compression ratios and two teacher architectures, with three seeds per configuration.
- An open evaluation harness that reports variance alongside every headline number.

## Method

### Alignment objective

Let $A^{(l)}$ denote the attention matrix at layer $l$ of a transformer. We minimise

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}} + \lambda \sum_{l \in \mathcal{M}} \mathrm{JSD}\!\left( A^{(l)}_{\mathrm{student}} \,\Vert\, A^{(l)}_{\mathrm{teacher}} \right)
$$

where $\mathcal{M}$ selects every third layer of the teacher and $\lambda = 0.15$ throughout. Jensen–Shannon divergence is used instead of KL because attention rows are often near-uniform early in training, which makes raw KL penalties explode.

### Training configuration

```python
config = {
    "teacher": "reference-base-110m",
    "student_width": 0.5,
    "alignment_layers": "every_third",
    "lambda": 0.15,
    "epochs": 6,
    "seeds": [13, 29, 61],
}
```

### Evaluation protocol

We report token-level accuracy, expected calibration error (ECE), and parameter count. Out-of-domain robustness is measured on a held-out corpus that is structurally different from the training distribution — poetry and source code — without any fine-tuning.

## Results

| Compression | Student params | Accuracy | ECE | Accuracy delta vs. baseline |
| ----------- | -------------- | -------- | --- | --------------------------- |
| 0.75        | 82M            | 91.2%    | 2.1 | +0.4                        |
| 0.50        | 55M            | 89.7%    | 2.4 | +0.9                        |
| 0.35        | 38M            | 86.1%    | 3.0 | +1.6                        |

Attention-aligned students lose less accuracy per parameter removed, and the gap widens as compression increases. On the out-of-domain split the aligned 0.50 student trails its teacher by 4.8 points where the token-level baseline trails by 9.3.

## Discussion

The alignment term helps most exactly where output-level matching is weakest: long-range dependencies and rare-token routing. Two limitations temper the result. First, alignment adds roughly 18 percent to distillation wall-clock time, which matters for practitioners distilling large teachers. Second, our teachers share a single architecture family with the students; cross-family transfer remains open.

We consider the calibration result the more interesting half: smaller models fail quietly, and an objective that keeps failure loud is worth its training cost.
