## Abstract

Sample content — this demonstration paper exists to exercise the PaperStack pipeline and will be replaced by real research. We show that the timing profile of a TLS handshake, collected passively at line rate, is enough to distinguish browser families with a 91 percent top-1 rate across 12 widely deployed clients. The signal survives TLS 1.3's encryption of most handshake fields, which makes timing a remaining, and largely unmitigated, fingerprinting surface.

## Introduction

TLS encrypts handshake certificates and, since 1.3, most extension data. What it does not change is _when_ work happens: certificate parsing, key schedule computation, and extension processing each leave a timing residue that a passive observer with packet-level timestamps can still collect.

Prior fingerprinting work concentrates on the plaintext ClientHello of TLS 1.2 and earlier. We ask whether the timing dimension alone carries enough signal to matter in a post-1.3 world.

We make three contributions:

- A passive measurement pipeline that extracts 14 timing features per handshake from one-sided packet captures.
- An evaluation across 12 client implementations on three network conditions.
- An analysis of which features carry signal after TLS 1.3, and why padding countermeasures miss most of them.

## Method

### Data collection

We captured 48,000 handshakes over six weeks across three vantage points, balancing residential and datacenter paths. Each capture records only timestamps, directions, and sizes — no payload is retained.

### Features

For a handshake with inter-packet gaps $g_1, \dots, g_n$, the feature vector is

$$
\vec{f} = \left( \min_i g_i,\ \max_i g_i,\ \mathrm{med}_i\, g_i,\ \sigma_i\, g_i,\ \sum_i \mathbb{1}\!\left[g_i > \mu + 2\sigma\right],\ \dots \right)
$$

covering spread, skew, and outlier-gap counts in both directions.

### Classifier

A gradient-boosted tree classifier is trained per vantage point with client-family labels. Hyperparameters are fixed by a small grid search on a validation quarter of the data.

```python
features = [
    "gap_min", "gap_max", "gap_median", "gap_std",
    "outlier_count_up", "outlier_count_down",
    "cert_parse_delta", "finished_delta",
]
model = GradientBoostedTrees(
    n_estimators=400, max_depth=6, class_weight="balanced",
)
```

## Results

| Client family      | Handshakes | Top-1 | Top-3 | Median confidence |
| ------------------ | ---------- | ----- | ----- | ----------------- |
| Chromium-based     | 21,400     | 0.94  | 0.99  | 0.71              |
| Gecko-based        | 9,100      | 0.89  | 0.97  | 0.63              |
| WebKit-based       | 8,700      | 0.90  | 0.96  | 0.66              |
| Command-line tools | 8,800      | 0.87  | 0.94  | 0.58              |

Aggregated across families, top-1 accuracy is 0.91. Errors concentrate almost entirely inside families — telling two Chromium builds apart is harder than telling Chromium from Firefox — which is the privacy-relevant granularity for tracking.

## Discussion

Three results deserve emphasis. First, the certificate-parse delta — the gap between ServerHello and Certificate — is the single strongest feature, and TLS 1.3 neither encrypts nor pads the timing around it. Second, naive padding of the whole handshake removes under half the signal at a 9 percent throughput cost. Third, accuracy is stable across vantage points, so the attack does not require a privileged position on the client's path.

The responsible disclosure summary is simple: we reported the finding to two client vendors, both of which confirmed the behaviour and none of which currently plans a timing mitigation. A practical defence likely needs jitter deliberately larger than the inter-feature gaps, and we sketch the cost bounds for that in the report.
